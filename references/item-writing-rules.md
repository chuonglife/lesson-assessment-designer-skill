# Item-writing rules

Sources: *Guidance on writing written and multiple-choice tests* (Step 4); Module 06, Lesson 2 (requirements for essay, oral and selected-response questions). The original examples come from education studies and maths; the examples here are from courses for teachers and trainers, so swap in your learners' world.

## 1. Rules for every item

1. Assess **important** content, not trivia.
2. Match the test's presentation and point rules.
3. Show clearly the **content and cognitive level** being measured.
4. Say everything the writer intends; clear words and structure for every learner.
5. Fit the learners' level.
6. Ask for understanding more than memory.
7. **Never copy sentences** from the textbook, slides or narration.

## 2. Essay and short-answer questions

- Ask learners to use knowledge in a **new situation**.
- State one task and how to carry it out.
- Give the length, the time and the criteria.
- If you ask learners to take a position and argue it, say the answer is marked on the **logic of the argument**, not on the position.

In this skill, `short-answer` items are 5 to 15 minute application tasks: "Rewrite this objective with all 4 components", "Write 3 pause questions for this passage". Scoring is split into points worth 0.25 to 1, with a model answer that passes.

Example:
> Tom teaches air-conditioning repair at a college in Cardiff. His lesson objective reads: "Help learners understand the refrigerant charging procedure." Rewrite it with all 4 components (measurable verb, object, condition, standard). 1 or 2 sentences, 5 minutes. Scoring: measurable verb (0.5), concrete object (0.5), condition or tool (0.5), countable standard (0.5).

## 3. Oral and pause questions

1. Clear purpose. 2. Prepared in advance. 3. **Short**. 4. Fits the objective. 5. One idea per question. 6. Few pure-memory questions. 7. Prefer questions that make learners reason. 8. Clear, consistent, precise. 9. **No yes/no questions**. 10. The asker's attitude changes the answers.

Pause questions already written in a lesson are not rewritten here; their expected wrong answers are excellent distractors.

## 4. Selected-response items

### 4.1 True or false (`true-false`)

**Form**: one or more statements; learners decide true or false.

**Strengths**: easy to write with few technical mistakes; compact; fast to answer (3 to 4 a minute). **Weaknesses**: 50% guessing chance; answers leak easily; easy to write worthless items.

**Rules**:
- Do not copy sentences from the material.
- A false statement has **exactly one false element**.
- True or false must be certain and well founded, never ambiguous.
- Avoid giveaway words: "usually", "sometimes", "may" tend to mark true statements; "always", "never", "all", "none", "only" tend to mark false ones.
- Avoid long, detailed statements. If a statement has a main clause and a reason clause, put the false element in the **reason clause**.
- About as many true as false, in random order.
- True or false items carry no more than 20% of the test's points.

Original example: "A multiple-choice test covers the program more broadly than a written test." (true). Course example: "On Udemy, a quiz after a video only counts as passed when the learner gets 100% right." (false, one false element: 100%).

### 4.2 Multiple choice (`mcq`)

**Form**: a stem (a question or an incomplete statement) and usually 4 options; learners choose the one correct or best answer.

**Strengths**: lower guessing chance than true or false; separates levels of mastery well. **Weaknesses**: slow to write; more paper and time than true or false; learners can recognise the answer; no room for their own words.

**Rules**:
- Assess important content; follow the test's presentation and point rules.
- The stem asks a **direct question** or sets out a **concrete problem**.
- **Do not** copy sentences from the material.
- Clear words and structure for every learner.
- Every distractor is **plausible to a learner who has not mastered** the content.
- Build each distractor on a **real mistake or misconception**.
- The key of one item is **independent** of the others (no item gives away another).
- Options agree with the stem and with each other.
- Exactly **one** correct, best answer.
- **No** "all of the above" or "none of the above".
- The same number of options throughout the test (3 or 4; the default here is 4).

From practice: distractors about as long as the key; no word from the stem repeated only in the key; avoid negative stems, and when you need one write NOT or EXCEPT in capitals; spread the keys evenly over A to D across the test.

Original example: "Which test method requires the teacher to observe learners most closely? A. Oral test · B. Practical product test · C. Practical performance test · D. Multiple-choice test." (C).

Course example:
> Hannah, a Year 9 teacher in Leeds, writes this objective for her online course: "Learners will grasp how to ask open questions." Which fault should she fix first?
> A. The action has no object · B. The verb cannot be measured · C. There is no condition · D. It is written from the teacher's side.
> Key B. A is wrong because "how to ask open questions" is the object. C is a real fault, but it comes second: "grasp" already makes the objective uncheckable.

### 4.3 Matching (`matching`)

**Form**: an instruction saying what to match; two columns of **unequal length**: the left holds the prompts (numbered 1, 2, 3), the right holds the options (lettered A, B, C), with more options than prompts, usually about twice as many.

**Strengths**: a kind of multiple choice with a low guessing chance (3 prompts, 6 options: 1/6 x 1/5 x 1/4). **Weaknesses**: hard to write; takes space and time to write and to answer.

**Rules**:
- A clear instruction on how the columns relate.
- Items in one column are **of one kind**; each column has a **title**.
- Left numbered, right lettered; right about twice the left to limit guessing, but not so long that it wastes time.
- Each right-hand option matches **at most one** prompt.

Original example: match teaching methods (illustrated lecture, guided discussion, demonstration) with their purposes (6 options). Course examples: 3 objectives to 6 Bloom levels; 4 faults to 8 faulty objectives.

### 4.4 Fill in the blank (`fill-blank`)

**Form**: a statement with a blank for a word, number or formula.

**Strengths**: no guessing; easy to write; good for concepts, terms, names, places. **Weaknesses**: only checks isolated facts; slower to mark (auto-marking needs every accepted spelling).

**Rules**:
- Not so many blanks that the sentence loses its meaning.
- Blank **in the middle or at the end**.
- Fill in terms, symbols, formulas, numbers; **never a verb**.
- Basic knowledge, not trivia.
- Avoid wording that allows several right answers.
- Blanks of **equal length**; answers on the answer sheet.
- Do not copy sentences from the material.

Course example: "A complete learning objective has four components: an action verb, an object, a ____ and a ____." (condition; standard; also accept "condition or tool", "criterion").

## 5. Writing distractors from real mistakes

Where to look, in order:
1. The "common mistakes", "faults" or "watch out" parts of the lesson.
2. The wrong answers the lesson expects to its own pause questions ("if they say...", "many people answer...").
3. Concepts the lesson sets side by side (Understand and Apply; checklist and rubric; condition and standard).
4. The learners' old habits (teachers who write "aims", trainers who write "will understand").
5. Answers that are right somewhere else: another lesson, another context.

A distractor must **not** be: absurd, so everyone rules it out; right under some reasonable reading (that makes two keys); a joke.

## 6. Laying out the whole test

- Every item fits the objectives and the blueprint.
- Several item types in one test are grouped into **parts**, never shuffled together.
- Keys sit at random positions.
- Paper: at least **two forms** with the same items in a different order, plus an answer sheet. Screen: use the platform's shuffle.
- Every item carries the same points unless the test says otherwise.
- Answer sheet: a grid of item / A B C D (or T F), a box for the form, the name and the number correct.

## 7. Every check the script runs

**Blocking errors** (nothing is built):
- An item without an `objective`, or with an unknown code; an objective `measured_by` test or both with no item; one `measured_by` assignment or both with no assignment.
- A multiple-choice item without exactly one key; option count different from `options_per_item`; duplicate options; "all of the above", "none of the above", "both A and B", "A and C" and the like.
- A true or false statement with a giveaway word (always, never, all, none, every, only, sometimes, usually, often, generally, may, might, most); true or false over 20% of the points; with 4 or more true or false items, true and false counts more than one apart.
- A matching item with the right column not longer than the left; an option used twice; a missing column title.
- A fill-in item with no blank, more than 2 blanks, a blank at the start, or no accepted answers.
- A short-answer item without `scoring`, or scoring that does not add up to its points.
- Items plus assignments counted in the test not equal to `total_points`; selected-response items with different points.
- An unknown level; an item two or more levels below its objective.
- A long dash anywhere; a stock AI phrase anywhere.
- An assignment without objectives, criteria, task, deliverable, minutes or pass rule; criteria not adding up to the assignment's points; a rubric with fewer than 3 levels or a criterion without one descriptor per level.
- A selected-response item without `explanation`.

**Warnings** (built, read and decide):
- Key at least 1.5 times as long as the longest distractor (and over 25 characters).
- One letter used for over 40% of keys in a form, or three keys in a row the same (the script retries 60 shuffles first).
- A negative stem not in capitals.
- A stem under 6 or over 70 words.
- An item one level below an application objective; an item above its objective; no application item while an application objective is measured by the test.
- Fewer items than an objective's `min_items`.
- A right matching column less than twice the left (with 3 or more prompts).
- A rubric descriptor with nothing countable in it.
- No `source` on an item; no model answer on a short-answer item.
- A formative quiz over 12 items; a summative test under 15 items with no assignment.
